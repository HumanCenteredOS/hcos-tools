# HCOS™ Hybrid Resume Prompt
### Human-Centered + ATS Optimized

**Document ID:** HCOS-P-401
**Category:** Professional Prompt
**Status:** Draft
**Version:** 1.0.0
**Last Updated:** July 2026

---

## Purpose

To produce resumes that communicate effectively with both Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) and human reviewers while preserving honesty, professional credibility, and the human-centered principles of HCOS™.

The objective is not simply to maximize keyword matching, but to accurately communicate an applicant's qualifications, contributions, and potential value to patients, coworkers, organizations, and the systems in which they work.
# HCOS™ Hybrid Resume Prompt

## Human-Centered + ATS Optimized

Before writing, revising, or tailoring a resume, remember that every organization is a human system.

A resume should communicate not only what someone has done, but also how their work supported patients, coworkers, organizations, and the systems in which they worked.

The goal is not simply to pass an Applicant Tracking System, or ATS.

The goal is to help both computers and humans recognize the applicant’s qualifications, contributions, and professional value.

The strongest resume communicates effectively with both.

---

# Primary Objective

Create a resume that simultaneously:

* Performs well in ATS software
* Reads naturally for recruiters and hiring managers
* Demonstrates relevant professional and technical expertise
* Reflects human-centered and systems-based thinking
* Clearly connects work to meaningful outcomes
* Remains concise, credible, and easy to review
* Remains completely truthful

Never invent experience.

Never fabricate metrics, outcomes, responsibilities, software proficiency, certifications, leadership authority, or clinical expertise.

Never exaggerate accomplishments.

Never imply that the applicant led, managed, built, owned, supervised, implemented, or directed work unless that accurately reflects the applicant’s role.

Every statement should be defensible in an interview.

---

# Part 1 — Analyze the Target Position

Before drafting or revising the resume, review the target job description.

Identify:

* Required qualifications
* Preferred qualifications
* Major responsibilities
* Repeated keywords
* Technical terminology
* Clinical or industry terminology
* Software and systems
* Certifications and licenses
* Regulatory requirements
* Leadership expectations
* Operational responsibilities
* Collaboration requirements
* The problems the organization appears to be trying to solve

Distinguish between:

* Experience the applicant clearly possesses
* Experience that is related or transferable
* Experience the applicant does not possess

Do not disguise missing experience.

Do not rewrite transferable experience as direct experience.

Instead, accurately emphasize the most relevant aspects of the applicant’s actual background.

---

# Part 2 — Preserve ATS Performance

Use terminology that recruiters and ATS software are likely to recognize.

When truthful and relevant, naturally incorporate:

* Industry terminology
* Job-title language
* Technical skills
* Clinical competencies
* Software platforms
* Certifications
* Licenses
* Regulations
* Quality standards
* Methodologies
* Operational terminology
* Keywords found in the target position

Preserve exact terminology from the job description when it accurately describes the applicant’s experience.

Do not keyword stuff.

Do not place long strings of disconnected keywords into the resume merely to increase ATS scoring.

Keywords should appear naturally within:

* The professional summary
* Areas of expertise
* Technical skills
* Work experience
* Accomplishment bullets
* Education and certification sections

Use standard section headings that ATS software can recognize, such as:

* Professional Summary
* Areas of Expertise
* Professional Experience
* Education
* Certifications
* Licensure
* Technical Skills
* Professional Affiliations

Avoid overly creative headings that may confuse resume software.

---

# Part 3 — Preserve Human Meaning

A resume should not reduce a person to a list of tasks.

Whenever possible, each bullet should answer one or more of these questions:

* What problem did this work help solve?
* Who benefited?
* Why did the work matter?
* How did it improve care?
* How did it improve quality?
* How did it improve safety?
* How did it improve access?
* How did it reduce unnecessary work?
* How did it strengthen communication?
* How did it support collaboration?
* How did it improve patient experience?
* How did it improve continuity of care?
* How did it improve organizational effectiveness?
* How did it strengthen a workflow or system?
* How did it help others make better decisions?

Do not simply describe activity.

Describe contribution.

The contribution may involve direct outcomes, but it may also involve:

* Enabling other professionals
* Supporting safer decisions
* Preventing delays
* Clarifying information
* Improving coordination
* Identifying risk
* Reducing ambiguity
* Strengthening processes
* Supporting consistent practice
* Helping a team function more effectively

---

# Part 4 — Balance Technical and Human Language

Preserve the technical task while explaining its human or organizational value.

Instead of writing:

> Performed medication reconciliation.

Prefer:

> Performed comprehensive medication reconciliation to identify discrepancies, promote medication safety, and support coordinated patient care.

The ATS keyword remains.

The reason the work mattered becomes clear.

Instead of writing:

> Completed prior authorizations.

Prefer:

> Partnered with patients, providers, pharmacies, and payers to navigate prior authorizations and medication access requirements, helping reduce avoidable treatment delays for complex therapies.

The technical responsibility remains visible.

The human impact becomes understandable.

Instead of writing:

> Documented patient interactions.

Prefer:

> Maintained clear and timely clinical documentation to support continuity of care, interdisciplinary communication, and informed treatment decisions.

Do not add impact language that cannot reasonably be connected to the work.

Human-centered language should clarify the contribution, not decorate it.

---

# Part 5 — Explain Systems Thinking

Whenever truthful, describe work that strengthened systems rather than merely completing work within them.

Relevant examples may include:

* Improving workflows
* Reducing barriers
* Strengthening communication
* Supporting continuity of care
* Increasing consistency
* Improving documentation
* Enhancing collaboration
* Reducing unnecessary complexity
* Improving patient access
* Improving quality
* Promoting medication safety
* Supporting evidence-based practice
* Clarifying roles and responsibilities
* Identifying process gaps
* Improving handoffs
* Supporting technology adoption
* Translating clinical needs into operational requirements
* Helping teams navigate fragmented systems
* Strengthening coordination across departments
* Supporting reliable implementation
* Reducing avoidable rework
* Improving visibility into operational concerns

These ideas should emerge naturally from the applicant’s actual work.

Never force systems language into every bullet.

Not every responsibility needs to sound transformational.

Some responsibilities are valuable because they were performed consistently, accurately, safely, and compassionately.

---

# Part 6 — Ground Every Statement in Evidence

Every accomplishment should be supported by observable work.

Prefer describing:

* Actions performed
* Decisions supported
* Processes improved
* Patients or teams served
* Problems addressed
* Systems used
* Projects supported
* Responsibilities carried
* Collaboration provided
* Barriers reduced
* Workflows strengthened
* Documentation improved
* Care coordinated

Avoid unsupported statements such as:

* Improved patient outcomes
* Delivered exceptional care
* Increased efficiency
* Enhanced quality
* Transformed operations
* Drove innovation
* Produced significant savings
* Greatly improved performance

These statements may be used only when the resume explains how the result occurred and the applicant has reasonable evidence to support it.

Specificity builds credibility.

Instead of:

> Improved outcomes for specialty pharmacy patients.

Prefer:

> Provided clinical assessment, medication education, access support, and follow-up for patients receiving specialty therapies, helping support safe use, treatment continuity, and informed patient participation.

The revised statement explains the work without claiming an outcome that cannot be proven.

---

# Part 7 — Use Metrics When They Exist

Include measurable accomplishments when accurate data is available.

Relevant measures may include:

* Number of patients served
* Number of clinics or departments supported
* Disease states covered
* Prescription or referral volume
* Program reach
* Turnaround time
* Quality measures
* Adherence measures
* Cost savings
* Revenue impact
* Error reduction
* Workflow time reduction
* Implementation timelines
* Audit results
* Training volume
* Committee participation
* Geographic reach
* Number of systems or workflows supported

Use metrics only when they are known and defensible.

Never invent percentages, dollar amounts, patient counts, rankings, or savings.

Do not estimate unless the applicant has provided a reasonable estimate and wishes to identify it as approximate.

When metrics are unavailable, use concrete scope and context rather than fabricating numbers.

For example:

> Supported specialty pharmacy services across multiple clinics and complex disease states.

This is better than inventing a patient volume.

---

# Part 8 — Highlight Human Impact

Whenever supported by experience, demonstrate contributions such as:

* Strengthened interdisciplinary collaboration
* Improved patient understanding
* Reduced barriers to treatment
* Supported evidence-based care
* Optimized medication therapy
* Improved communication
* Enhanced workflow
* Improved continuity of care
* Promoted medication safety
* Supported quality improvement
* Improved access to medication
* Clarified clinical information
* Supported informed decision-making
* Reduced avoidable treatment disruption
* Helped patients navigate complex systems
* Supported colleagues through education and consultation
* Improved consistency across teams
* Helped technology better reflect clinical workflows

Focus on the value of the work rather than self-promotion.

Do not describe the applicant as compassionate, collaborative, strategic, or patient-centered without showing evidence through actions.

Show the quality through the work.

---

# Part 9 — Protect Readability

Write for someone who may initially spend only a few seconds reviewing the resume.

Use:

* Clear section headings
* Concise paragraphs
* Focused bullet points
* Grouped expertise sections
* Logical organization
* Consistent formatting
* Adequate white space
* Short, meaningful phrases
* Strong opening words
* Easily recognized dates and job titles

Avoid:

* Dense walls of text
* Overly long summaries
* Bullets containing multiple unrelated ideas
* Excessive jargon
* Repetitive statements
* Decorative formatting that may interfere with ATS parsing
* Tables, text boxes, columns, graphics, icons, or skill bars when ATS compatibility is a priority
* Small fonts or crowded margins
* First-person pronouns
* Long lists of generic soft skills

Each section should immediately communicate relevant value.

Most bullets should generally remain between one and three lines, depending on the resume format.

Prioritize clarity over compression.

---

# Part 10 — Maintain Professional Tone

Write with:

* Truth without exaggeration
* Confidence without arrogance
* Professionalism without impersonality
* Achievement without self-importance
* Humility without minimizing expertise
* Technical precision without unnecessary complexity
* Human meaning without sentimentality

Avoid buzzwords such as:

* Rockstar
* Ninja
* Guru
* Visionary
* Game changer
* Superstar
* World-class
* Disruptor
* Thought leader

Avoid unsupported corporate clichés such as:

* Results-driven professional
* Proven track record of success
* Dynamic self-starter
* Strategic thinker
* Passionate change agent
* Synergy-focused leader
* Works well independently and as part of a team

Replace vague claims with specific evidence.

---

# Part 11 — Tailor the Resume to the Position

Do not use the same emphasis for every application.

For each target position:

1. Identify the five to ten most important competencies.
2. Match those competencies to truthful experience.
3. Prioritize the most relevant experience.
4. Adjust the professional summary.
5. Refine the areas of expertise.
6. Reorder bullets when appropriate.
7. Incorporate relevant terminology naturally.
8. Reduce emphasis on unrelated experience.
9. Preserve important career history without allowing it to distract from the target role.
10. Clearly distinguish direct experience from transferable experience.

Do not remove valuable experience solely because it is not an exact match.

Instead, translate the relevant contribution accurately.

For example, work with health-system workflows may be relevant to a healthcare technology role even when the applicant did not hold a formal technology title.

Explain the connection without overstating it.

---

# Part 12 — Prioritize the Highest-Value Information

Place the most relevant and persuasive information first.

Within the professional summary:

* Lead with the applicant’s professional identity
* State years or depth of experience when accurate
* Identify the most relevant areas of expertise
* Include important credentials
* Explain the type of value the applicant brings
* Avoid turning the summary into a biography

Within each position:

* Begin with the responsibilities or contributions most relevant to the target role
* Place high-value accomplishments before routine duties
* Group related responsibilities together
* Remove unnecessary repetition
* Give greater detail to recent and relevant positions
* Use less detail for older or less relevant roles

Within the expertise section:

* List the strongest job-relevant competencies first
* Use terminology the target employer will recognize
* Avoid generic qualities that cannot be verified

A recruiter should not need to search for the applicant’s most relevant qualifications.

---

# Part 13 — Protect Accuracy of Role and Authority

Use verbs that accurately reflect the applicant’s level of responsibility.

Use terms such as:

* Led
* Directed
* Managed
* Oversaw
* Owned
* Built
* Designed
* Implemented
* Approved
* Supervised

only when the applicant truly held that authority.

When the applicant contributed without formal ownership, consider language such as:

* Supported
* Contributed to
* Participated in
* Collaborated on
* Helped develop
* Provided clinical input for
* Served as a subject matter resource
* Assisted with implementation
* Validated
* Tested
* Coordinated
* Facilitated
* Recommended
* Advised
* Identified
* Evaluated

Do not minimize meaningful contribution.

Accurate language can still demonstrate expertise.

For example:

> Provided clinical and operational input during workflow testing and system validation.

This may be more truthful than:

> Led system implementation.

Precision protects both the applicant and the employer.

---

# Part 14 — Address Transferable Experience Honestly

When the applicant lacks direct experience in one area but has related experience:

* Identify the overlapping competency
* Explain the connection clearly
* Preserve the distinction between direct and transferable experience
* Avoid language that creates a false equivalence

Examples of transferable competencies may include:

* Clinical judgment
* Workflow analysis
* Stakeholder communication
* Technology adoption
* Documentation improvement
* Quality improvement
* Patient access
* Regulatory interpretation
* Training
* Process validation
* Cross-functional collaboration
* Change support
* Operational problem-solving

Do not claim formal implementation leadership because the applicant supported implementation.

Do not claim software build experience because the applicant tested or used the system.

Do not claim management experience because the applicant mentored, coordinated, or influenced peers.

Use the strongest accurate description.

---

# Part 15 — Create Strong Resume Bullets

Each bullet should ideally contain three elements:

**Action + Context + Value**

Example:

> Reviewed complex specialty medication regimens and relevant clinical information to support evidence-based therapy decisions, patient safety, and continuity of care.

Action:

> Reviewed

Context:

> Complex specialty medication regimens and relevant clinical information

Value:

> Supported evidence-based therapy decisions, patient safety, and continuity of care

Not every bullet requires all three elements, but the resume should consistently explain why the work mattered.

Begin bullets with clear verbs.

Avoid repeatedly using the same opening verb.

Do not begin every bullet with “Responsible for.”

Avoid overloading one bullet with several separate responsibilities.

Split bullets when doing so improves clarity.

---

# Part 16 — Optimize the Professional Summary

The professional summary should be concise, specific, and tailored.

It should communicate:

* Professional identity
* Relevant years or depth of experience
* Core areas of expertise
* Important credentials
* Relevant systems, populations, or environments
* The type of contribution the applicant brings

The summary should not:

* Repeat the entire resume
* Contain vague personality claims
* Overstate leadership
* Include unsupported achievements
* Become a personal mission statement
* Use first-person language
* Become so human-centered that technical expertise is lost

Preferred structure:

> [Professional identity] with [depth of experience] in [primary relevant areas]. Experienced in [technical, clinical, or operational competencies]. Known for supporting [relevant human, clinical, or organizational value] through [specific types of contribution].

---

# Part 17 — Build a Meaningful Areas of Expertise Section

Create an expertise section using terms relevant to the target position.

Include a balanced combination of:

* Technical skills
* Clinical skills
* Operational skills
* Regulatory knowledge
* Software knowledge
* Collaboration competencies
* Quality or systems competencies

Do not use the expertise section as a dumping ground for every possible keyword.

Do not include qualities such as:

* Hard worker
* Team player
* Motivated
* Dependable
* Compassionate
* Detail-oriented

unless the resume demonstrates them elsewhere through evidence.

Use recognizable professional terminology.

---

# Part 18 — Format for ATS Compatibility

Unless the user requests a more visual version, use an ATS-compatible format.

Use:

* One-column layout
* Standard fonts
* Standard section headings
* Consistent date formatting
* Simple bullet points
* Plain text contact information
* Recognizable job titles
* Clear employer names
* Standard file formats such as DOCX or PDF, depending on application instructions

Avoid placing essential information in:

* Headers
* Footers
* Text boxes
* Graphics
* Logos
* Icons
* Charts
* Tables
* Images
* Sidebars

Do not rely on color to communicate meaning.

Do not use abbreviations without writing out important terms at least once when space allows.

For example:

> Medication Therapy Management (MTM)

Use the full term and abbreviation naturally.

---

# Part 19 — Remove Redundancy

Review the resume for repeated concepts.

Avoid repeating the same idea in:

* The professional summary
* Areas of expertise
* Multiple bullets
* Several different positions

Repetition is acceptable when the same competency was central to multiple roles, but each bullet should provide different context or evidence.

For example, “interdisciplinary collaboration” may appear in several roles, but each occurrence should explain a distinct contribution.

Remove filler that does not strengthen qualification, clarity, or credibility.

---

# Part 20 — Optimize for Both Audiences

Every section must pass two tests.

## ATS Test

Can software clearly recognize:

* Professional experience
* Relevant job titles
* Certifications
* Licenses
* Education
* Technical expertise
* Required competencies
* Healthcare or industry terminology
* Software proficiency
* Regulatory knowledge
* Relevant keywords

## Human Test

Can a recruiter or hiring manager understand:

* What this person actually does
* What expertise this person brings
* How this person thinks
* How this person collaborates
* How this person improves care or operations
* How this person responds to complexity
* How this person contributes to healthier systems
* Why this person would make a valuable colleague
* Whether the applicant’s experience is credible
* Whether the applicant understands the needs of the role

If either test fails, revise.

# Final HCOS™ Resume Review

Before producing the final resume, evaluate it from the following perspectives.

## 1. Accuracy

Ask:

* Is every statement truthful?
* Has any responsibility been exaggerated?
* Has any metric been invented?
* Does every leadership verb accurately reflect the applicant’s authority?
* Are direct and transferable experiences clearly distinguished?
* Could the applicant confidently explain every bullet in an interview?

If not, revise.

## 2. ATS Performance

Ask:

* Are the most important job-description keywords naturally included?
* Will ATS software recognize the applicant’s credentials?
* Are technical skills clearly named?
* Are standard section headings used?
* Is the format easy for software to parse?
* Are abbreviations supported by full terminology where appropriate?
* Is important information placed outside headers, footers, tables, and graphics?

If not, revise.

## 3. Hiring Manager Perspective

Ask:

* Can someone quickly understand the applicant’s professional identity?
* Are the strongest qualifications visible near the top?
* Does the resume explain what the applicant contributed?
* Does it show why the work mattered?
* Does it clearly connect the applicant’s background to the target position?
* Is the writing concise and credible?
* Would the applicant appear prepared to discuss the experience in an interview?

If not, revise.

## 4. Human-Centered Perspective

Ask:

* Does the resume communicate respect for patients, colleagues, and organizations?
* Does it show how the applicant strengthened care, collaboration, access, quality, safety, or workflow?
* Does it acknowledge the systems in which work occurs?
* Does it demonstrate contribution without excessive self-promotion?
* Does it preserve the applicant’s dignity without turning the resume into a personal narrative?
* Does it communicate technical excellence and human meaning together?

If not, revise.

## 5. Readability

Ask:

* Is the resume easy to scan?
* Are sections organized logically?
* Are bullets concise?
* Is unnecessary repetition removed?
* Is the most relevant experience emphasized?
* Is there adequate white space?
* Are long sentences simplified?
* Does every section add meaningful information?

If not, revise.

---

# Final Quality Check

Before presenting the resume, confirm:

✓ Every statement is truthful.

✓ No experience, authority, software proficiency, metric, or result has been invented.

✓ Important ATS keywords are naturally included.

✓ Required qualifications are clearly visible.

✓ Transferable experience is described honestly.

✓ Each section communicates meaningful value.

✓ The resume explains not only what the applicant did, but why it mattered.

✓ Technical expertise remains clear and specific.

✓ Human-centered language is supported by evidence.

✓ The strongest information appears first.

✓ The resume is readable, concise, and ATS compatible.

✓ The applicant’s contributions are described confidently without exaggeration.

✓ Both an ATS and a hiring manager can recognize why the applicant is qualified.

If any answer is no, continue refining.

---

# Required Output

When creating or revising the resume, provide:

1. A tailored professional headline
2. A concise professional summary
3. A targeted areas of expertise section
4. A technical skills section when relevant
5. Revised professional experience bullets
6. Education
7. Certifications and licensure
8. Relevant professional affiliations, teaching, publications, or service when appropriate
9. A brief list of important job-description keywords incorporated
10. A brief accuracy note identifying any experience that was intentionally not claimed
11. Optional recommendations for information that could strengthen the resume, such as verified metrics or missing project details

Do not include commentary inside the resume itself.

Keep resume content ready to copy into a professional document.

---

# HCOS™ Guiding Principle

A resume should never reduce a person to a list of tasks.

Nor should it become so narrative that it loses technical precision.

The strongest resume communicates competence, contribution, and character together.

Technical language helps organizations recognize the applicant’s qualifications.

Human-centered language helps them recognize the person behind those qualifications.

The strongest resumes communicate both with clarity, honesty, and purpose.
